Pointing your phone camera at a QR code is safe on its own. The camera reads the text inside the code and shows it to you, usually as a link at the bottom of the screen, and nothing opens until you tap. The risk is what you tap into: a fake login or payment page, or a page that pushes you to install something. So before you tap, read the address. If the domain isn't the business you expected, if it's a short link you can't see through, or if the code is a sticker stuck on top of a sign, don't open it. And never pay or log in through a page you reached from a code you aren't sure about. Go to the real website or app yourself instead.

If you put QR codes in front of customers, the second half of this guide is for you: a few plain choices make your code easy to trust.

## Key facts

- Scanning only reads the code. Your phone shows the link or action and waits for you to tap it.
- The FBI, the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security and city parking agencies all warn about the same trick: a fake code, often a sticker placed over a real one, that leads to a page asking for card details or a login.
- In Canada the problem is still small. The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre told CBC News in July 2025 it had about 10 reports of QR code phishing since 2024. Montreal and Ottawa had both recently warned about fake codes on parking signs and meters.
- The checks that matter: read the domain, look for a sticker, be extra careful with payment and login pages, and use the camera app that came with your phone rather than a scanner app from an ad.

## What to check before you tap

1. **Read the address the camera shows.** On an iPhone the link appears at the bottom of the camera screen and opens only when you tap it ([Apple's guide](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/scan-a-qr-code-iphe8bda8762/ios)). Android cameras and Google Lens show the link in the same way. Look at the domain, the part just before the first single slash. `example.com/menu` is example.com. `example.com.pay-now.net/menu` is pay-now.net.
2. **Watch for lookalikes.** A swapped or extra letter, `rn` instead of `m`, or the right brand name tacked onto a strange domain. The FBI's advice is to check the URL for exactly this kind of typo.
3. **Feel the code.** On a parking meter, a poster or a restaurant table, run a fingernail over the edge. A sticker on top of a printed sign is the most common real-world swap.
4. **Ask who put it there.** A code printed into a menu, a receipt or an official sign has an owner. A lone code taped to a pole, or one in an email or text you didn't expect, doesn't. Treat those like any unexpected link.
5. **Don't pay or log in from a code you're unsure of.** Calgary Parking's guide says it plainly: you don't need a QR code to pay. Open the real app or type the website yourself.
6. **Don't install apps from a code.** The FBI suggests getting apps from your phone's app store, and using the camera's built-in scanner instead of downloading a scanner app.

## What about short links?

Many legitimate codes hold a short link instead of the final address: the QR company's own domain plus a few letters, such as `qrco.de/...`, `bit.ly/...` or our own `scst.one/...`. That's how editable (dynamic) codes work. The printed code always holds the short link, and the company forwards each scan to wherever the owner points it today.

The downside is that the camera can't show you where the short link ends up. That's why some security guides tell people to be wary of shorteners. A short link on a code printed in a restaurant's own menu is very different from one on a sticker in a parking lot, so judge it by where the code is and who printed it. If you still aren't sure, open it and look at the address bar before you type anything into the page.

## If you already opened a bad page

- Close it. Don't enter anything else.
- If you typed a password, change it, and change it anywhere else you used the same one.
- If you entered card or bank details, call your bank or card company now and watch your statements.
- In Canada, report it to the [Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre](https://reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca/) (1-888-495-8501). In the US, report it to the FBI's [IC3](https://www.ic3.gov/). If the code was on a parking meter or a city sign, tell the city too, so they can take the sticker down.

## For businesses: make your QR code easy to trust

People have good reasons to hesitate. One reply on X in November 2026 said QR menus won't work "because you can't trust scanning anything." You can't fix that for every code out there, but you can make yours pass the checks above.

- **Point the code at your own domain where you can.** When the camera shows `yourrestaurant.com/menu`, people can see it's you before they tap. A static code does this: your address is stored in the pattern itself, with no QR company in between.
- **Print the address under the code.** Something like "Menu: yourrestaurant.com/menu". People who don't want to scan can type it, and people who do can see the code matches.
- **Print the code into the sign, not on a sticker.** If customers are told to check for stickers, don't make your real code a sticker.
- **Check your codes now and then.** Scan the ones in public spots, like parking, entrances and posters, and look for anything stuck on top.
- **Never ask for payment or a login on the first screen.** A menu, a schedule or a WiFi network doesn't need either, and a code that opens to a card form looks exactly like the scam people are warned about.
- **Keep the page plain.** No app download, no cookie wall, no "enter your phone number". Our [QR menu complaints guide](/blog/qr-code-menu-complaints) covers the menu version of this.
- **Need to edit it later?** Then you need a dynamic code, which means a short link. Use one from a company that sends scans straight to your page with no ad or preview page in between, and still print your own address under it. See [why some QR codes stop working after a free trial](/blog/qr-code-subscription-trap) for what to check before you pick one.

## Common questions

### Is it safe to scan a QR code?

Scanning on its own is safe: the camera only reads the code and shows you the link or action, and nothing opens until you tap. The risk is the page you open. Check the domain before you tap, and don't pay or log in from a code you aren't sure about.

### Can a QR code hack your phone just by scanning it?

Reading the code doesn't run anything. Your phone shows what's inside and asks before it opens a link, joins a network or adds a contact. The danger comes after you tap, from a fake page that asks for details or tries to get you to install something. Keeping your phone updated and getting apps only from the app store covers most of that.

### How can I tell where a QR code goes before I open it?

Point your camera at it and read the link it shows, without tapping. Look at the domain, the part before the first single slash, and check it matches the business you expect. If it's a short link on another company's domain, you can't see the final page until you open it, so judge it by where the code is printed.

### How do I spot a fake QR code on a parking meter or sign?

Look and feel for a sticker on top of the printed sign, a code that's crooked, the wrong size or a different style from the rest of the sign, and a link that doesn't match the city or parking company. If anything is off, don't scan it. Pay with the official app, the machine or the website you type in yourself.

### What should I do if I scanned a scam QR code?

Close the page. If you typed a password, change it everywhere you use it. If you entered card or bank details, call your bank right away. In Canada report it to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre, and in the US to the FBI's IC3.

### How can my business make QR codes people trust?

Point the code at your own domain where possible, print the address under the code, print the code into the sign rather than as a sticker, and don't ask for payment or a login on the first screen. Scan your public codes now and then to check nobody has stuck a different one on top.
